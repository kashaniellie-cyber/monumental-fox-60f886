The science of calm

Why the eye rests in a well-made pattern.

Richard Taylor is a physicist at the University of Oregon. His path into this field began with art: in 1999 he showed, in the journal Nature, that Jackson Pollock’s poured paintings are built from fractals — the same kind of pattern found in trees, clouds and coastlines.

That raised a larger question: why do these patterns feel so good to look at? Working with psychologists and neuroscientists, Taylor has spent more than twenty years measuring the answer — in skin, in brainwaves and in the movement of the eye itself. He calls the effect fractal fluency.

First, what is a fractal?

A fractal is a pattern that repeats at finer and finer scales: a branch splits into smaller branches, which split again. Scientists measure it with a single number, D — the fractal dimension — running from 1 (a smooth line) to 2 (a surface completely filled with detail).

Most natural scenes fall in a middle band, between about 1.3 and 1.5. That band, again and again, is where the research finds the strongest response.

What the studies found

  1. 60% The body recovers In a NASA-funded study, people performed stressful tasks, then viewed images while their skin conductance — a direct readout of the nervous system — was measured. With mid-range fractals in view, the stress response was reported to fall by around 60%.
  2. D 1.3 The brain is calm and alert An EEG study published in Perception (2008) found that D 1.3 patterns produced the strongest response: the most alpha waves in the frontal lobes, linked to wakeful relaxation, and the most beta waves in the parietal area, linked to attention. Relaxed and engaged at once.
  3. D 1.4 The eye already moves this way Eye-tracking shows that when we search a scene, our gaze traces a fractal path of its own — long sweeps, then smaller passes — at about D 1.4, much like the flight of a foraging bird. A pattern in the same range matches how we already look.
  4. 95% Almost everyone prefers it Around 95% of people chose fractal images over versions with the fractal content reduced. The preference for the middle range is already present in children by the age of two.
  5. 1.6–1.7 The room changes the answer When patterns cover the walls of a room, people prefer a richer pattern, around D 1.6–1.7. Plain, straight-edged surroundings make the eye want more detail, not less.

From the laboratory to the floor

The research has already left the lab. In 2019, the commercial flooring maker Mohawk Group launched Relaxing Floors, a carpet collection developed with the Austrian design studio 13&9 and Taylor’s company, Fractals Research. Its patterns were drawn from the paths our eyes trace across natural scenery and from the branching of neurons in the eye.

Taylor’s team has also developed fractal window blinds that cast fractal shadows, patterned ceiling tiles and fractal solar panels. The direction is clear: pattern is moving from decoration toward wellbeing — in offices, hospitals, hotels and homes.

One pattern does not fit everyone

Taylor is careful on this point. Roughly a quarter of people are drawn to more intricate patterns, and roughly a quarter to smoother ones; age and whether someone grew up in a city or the countryside also play a part. He also finds that adapting nature works better than copying it literally.

Some of the brain-imaging work is still described as preliminary. But the core finding has held across skin, brainwaves, eye movement and preference: the middle range, where nature lives, is where people are most at ease.

How GeoMera Atelier applies it

  1. 1Design across scales.A single compass construction is not a fractal on its own. Nesting a motif within itself — a star within a star, as the craftsmen of Isfahan did at the Darb-i Imam — gives the eye detail at every distance, from across the lobby to arm’s length.
  2. 2Tune the pattern to the room.A minimal marble lobby or stone bathroom can carry a richer pattern; a room already full of texture asks for a quieter one.
  3. 3Tune it to the person.Because people differ, a private commission can be calibrated to whether a client is drawn to calm simplicity or to intricacy.
  4. 4Calm without staring.The research suggests the effect does not depend on looking directly at a pattern — which is exactly how floors, walls and curtains are experienced.

GeoMera Atelier’s own patterns have not been tested in a laboratory. These are the principles by which we apply the published research.
